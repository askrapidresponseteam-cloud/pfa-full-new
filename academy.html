PFA AcademyFoundations: how to read a medicine

Most harm done to animals with medicine in India is not done by the wrong drug. It is done by the right drug in the wrong strength, by the wrong route, for the wrong species, or from a bottle that spent a summer in a car. This module gives you the vocabulary to avoid all four.

  • Tell a brand name from the molecule inside it and read a strength off any label.
  • Decode a prescription line, including the abbreviations vets use.
  • Say why a cat, a dog, a cow and a horse each need a different rule book.
  • Store vaccines and medicines so that they still work when needed.
1.1What is actually in the box: brand, molecule and strength

Every medicine has one true name, the generic or molecule name, and any number of brand names. The molecule is what acts; the brand is what the shop stocks.

Do this
Do thisBefore giving anything, read three things aloud: the molecule, the strength per tablet or per mL, and the weight of the animal. If any of the three is missing, stop and ask.

Meloxicam is meloxicam whether the carton says Melonex, Metacam or a chemist's own brand. The dangerous variable is not the brand but the strength: how many milligrams of the molecule sit in one tablet or one millilitre. Human meloxicam tablets are 7.5 mg and 15 mg. The veterinary oral suspension for dogs is 1.5 mg per mL, and the injection is usually 5 mg per mL. A 5 kg dog on a correct 0.1 mg per kg dose needs 0.5 mg. One human 7.5 mg tablet is fifteen times that.

The same is true of the antibiotic amoxicillin-clavulanate, which comes as 375 mg and 625 mg human tablets, veterinary 250 mg and 500 mg tablets, and paediatric syrups of several strengths. Reading "give one tablet" without reading which tablet is how overdoses happen.

1.2Reading an Indian label: Rx, Schedule H, H1, expiry, withdrawal

Indian labels carry a small set of legally fixed phrases. Once you can read them, a strip of tablets tells you how it may be bought, how long it lasts and, for a farm animal, how long its milk or meat must be discarded.

Warning6 facts
CarefulA strip with the Schedule H warning that a shop hands over without a prescription is a shop breaking the law, not a sign the drug is safe to use casually. The warning is on the strip because of what the drug can do.
"Schedule H drug. Warning: To be sold by retail on the prescription of a Registered Medical Practitioner only." Prescription only. Most antibiotics, NSAIDs, steroids and sedatives carry it. The definition of a registered practitioner in the Drugs Rules includes a person engaged in the practice of veterinary medicine, which is why a vet's prescription is enough at a chemist.
Schedule H1, a stricter class introduced in 2014, covers third and fourth generation cephalosporins, carbapenems, anti-tuberculosis drugs and a number of habit-forming drugs. The chemist must record the prescriber, the patient and the drug in a register kept for three years. If a vet prescribes one, expect the pharmacist to ask for the prescription and write it down.
"For veterinary use only" or "For animal treatment only" marks a veterinary licence. It does not make the drug gentler; it means the strength and label were set for animals.
Mfg., Exp., Batch No.Expiry is the last day the manufacturer guarantees potency under the stated storage. A vaccine or an antibiotic past expiry is not a bargain, it is a coin toss.
Withdrawal period on livestock medicines: the number of days after the last dose during which milk must not be sold and the animal must not be slaughtered for food. FSSAI sets residue limits for antibiotics in food; the withdrawal period is how a farmer stays under them.
Storage"Store below 25 °C", "Store at 2 °C to 8 °C, do not freeze", "Protect from light". In an Indian May, "below 25 °C" means a refrigerator or an air-conditioned room, not a shelf.
Basis
1.3Reading a prescription line, and the abbreviations vets use

A complete prescription line answers seven questions: which animal, what weight, which molecule, what strength, how much, by which route, how often and for how long. If one is missing, the line is not complete.

Table

An example, as it might be written for a 12 kg community dog with tick fever: Doxycycline 100 mg tab, 1/2 tab PO BID x 28 days, with food. Read it as: doxycycline, 100 mg tablets, half a tablet, by mouth, twice a day, for twenty-eight days, given with food. Half of 100 mg is 50 mg, which for 12 kg is about 4.2 mg per kg twice daily, inside the published range of 5 mg per kg twice a day or 10 mg per kg once a day. The arithmetic is checkable, which is the point of writing it out.

1.4Routes: why the same drug is a different drug by mouth, under the skin and in a vein

The route decides how fast a medicine arrives, how much of it arrives, and what it can damage on the way. Changing the route changes the dose, so a route is never a detail to improvise.

Warning6 facts
CarefulNever give an injectable by mouth or an oral liquid by injection, never inject anything into a vein yourself, and never put anything into the mouth of an animal that is unconscious, fitting or vomiting: it goes into the lungs.
By mouth (PO)slowest, safest for a layperson, and the only route a caregiver should ordinarily use. Some drugs are destroyed in the stomach or the rumen, which is why a cow's oral dose can differ so much from a dog's.
Under the skin (SC)most vaccines and fluids for dehydration. Absorbed over minutes to hours. Some irritant drugs cause an abscess or a slough if given this way.
Into muscle (IM)faster than SC; painful for some drugs; a wrong site in a hind leg can hit the sciatic nerve.
Into a vein (IV)immediate and complete. Antivenom, anaesthetics, calcium for milk fever and most emergency drugs go IV, and an IV drug given too fast, or a drug never meant for a vein, can stop a heart. This route belongs to a vet.
Topical, spot-on, pour-on, diptick and flea products, wound antiseptics. Dosed by weight band and, for cattle, along the back. What is safe on a dog's skin can kill the cat that grooms him.
Intramammarymastitis tubes, into the teat canal, after cleaning the teat end. Milk from that quarter is discarded for the label period.
Basis
1.5A cat is not a small dog, and a cow is not a large one

Species differ in the enzymes that break drugs down, in the way their guts work and in which organs a drug reaches. The differences are large enough to be fatal, and they are the reason the word "safe" never travels between species.

Do this5 facts
Do thisWhen you call a vet about a medicine, say the species first, then the weight, then the drug. A vet cannot give you a safe answer without the first word.
Cats have very little of the liver enzyme (glucuronyl transferase) that dogs and humans use to detoxify many drugs. Paracetamol, aspirin, permethrin, benzyl alcohol, phenolic disinfectants such as Dettol and many essential oils are dangerous to cats at doses that dogs tolerate. Cats also cannot make their own taurine or vitamin A and must not be fed as small dogs.
Dogs are the species most often poisoned by human painkillers because they share our homes. Ibuprofen, diclofenac and naproxen cause stomach ulcers and kidney failure at doses that look small. Some herding breeds carry a gene defect (MDR1, also written ABCB1) that lets ivermectin and related drugs into the brain.
Cattle, buffalo, goats and sheep are ruminants: an oral drug meets a forty-litre fermentation vat first, so many drugs are given by injection or as drenches designed for the rumen. Anything that stops the rumen (a wrong antibiotic, a fever, a plastic bag) becomes its own emergency. And whatever they receive can end up in milk.
Horses and donkeys cannot vomit and have a gut that twists easily; several antibiotics that are routine in dogs cause fatal colitis in horses. Tetanus is a far greater risk in equines than in any other domestic species.
Birds and rabbits have their own lists; a drug that is a standard antibiotic in a dog can kill a rabbit's gut flora and the rabbit with it.
Basis
1.6Who may prescribe, who may give, and what a layperson may do

The Indian Veterinary Council Act 1984 reserves the practice of veterinary medicine to registered veterinary practitioners, with a narrow allowance for State-permitted minor veterinary services. A rescuer may give first aid and may give a medicine a vet has prescribed, exactly as prescribed.

4 facts
Recognise an emergency, give first aid, and move the animal to a vet safely (Module 07).
Give medicines a vet has prescribed, at the right dose, route, time and duration, and check the arithmetic (Module 08).
Apply licensed over-the-counter products on the label's terms: a deworming tablet at the label dose, a tick spot-on for the right species and weight band, an antiseptic.
Keep records: weights, dates, batch numbers, what was given and how the animal responded. Records make the next vet visit shorter and safer.

Section 57 of the Act makes it an offence for anyone else to practise veterinary medicine, and the definition of veterinary medicine is wide: modern scientific veterinary medicine in all its branches, including surgery and obstetrics. Choosing a drug, a dose and a course for an animal is practice. Following a prescription is not.

What a caregiver may properly do, and what this Academy is written to help with:

Community dog vaccination, sterilisation and treatment programmes run under the Animal Birth Control Rules 2023 are carried out by or under veterinarians for the same reason: the medicines are safe in trained hands and the animals cannot speak for themselves.

Basis
1.7Storage in Indian heat: the cold chain, the eight-hour rule, the car

Biologicals die quietly. A vaccine that spent an afternoon at 40 °C looks exactly like one that did not, and the animal it fails to protect finds out months later.

Warning6 facts
CarefulA parked car in an Indian summer exceeds 60 °C inside. Nothing biological survives it. A rescue kit that lives in a vehicle should hold no vaccines and no antivenom, only what the label allows to be kept warm.
Vaccines live at 2 °C to 8 °C, in the body of a refrigerator, never the door and never the freezer compartment. Liquid (adsorbed) vaccines are destroyed by freezing; freeze-dried ones survive it but their diluent ampoules crack. Carry them in an insulated box with ice packs that do not touch the vials.
Reconstituted vaccine is used at once. The national rabies guidelines set eight hours at 2 °C to 8 °C as the absolute limit for an opened, reconstituted vial, after which it is discarded.
Antivenomthe freeze-dried form keeps for five years in a cool dark place; the liquid form needs 2 °C to 8 °C, keeps two years, and must not be frozen.
Insulin, some eye drops, probiotics, hyperimmune serarefrigerated, and the label says so.
Tablets and capsulesdry, dark, below 25 °C. Blister strips are not airtight once one pocket is broken.
Oral suspensionsshake before every dose. The drug settles; the first spoonful from an unshaken bottle is water and the last is a triple dose.

Module 01
A vet writes "Meloxicam susp 1.5 mg/mL, 0.1 mg/kg OD" for a 10 kg dog. A relative offers a human 7.5 mg meloxicam tablet, saying it is the same medicine. Is it the same dose?

10 kg × 0.1 mg/kg = 1 mg, which is 0.67 mL of the 1.5 mg/mL suspension. A 7.5 mg tablet is 7.5 times the dose, and half of it is still nearly four times. Same molecule, different strength, different dose.

Which sentence about the Schedule H warning is true?

Schedule H is the prescription-only class under the Drugs Rules 1945. The definition of a registered medical practitioner in the Rules includes a person engaged in the practice of veterinary medicine with approved qualifications.

A reconstituted rabies vaccine vial was opened at 9 am and kept in the refrigerator. It is now 7 pm. Can it be used?

The national rabies guidelines allow a reconstituted vial to be used for a maximum of eight hours at 2 °C to 8 °C. Ten hours is past the limit, refrigerated or not.

PFA AcademyMedicines: the classes you will meet

A prescription for a street dog or a village cow in India draws on a short list of drug classes. Knowing what each class does, and what it is known to break, is most of what a caregiver needs to follow a treatment intelligently and to notice when something is going wrong.

  • Name what a painkiller, a steroid, a dewormer and an antiemetic each do, and their signature risks.
  • Explain why four veterinary painkillers are banned in India and which one is used instead.
  • Recognise the six conditions that account for most sick street dogs and what treating them involves.
2.1Pain and inflammation: NSAIDs, and the four that India has banned for animals

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) block the enzymes that make prostaglandins, which is how they take away pain, fever and swelling. The same prostaglandins protect the stomach lining and keep blood flowing through the kidney, which is where every NSAID does its damage.

Warning
CarefulHuman NSAIDs are not a substitute. Ibuprofen causes stomach ulcers in dogs at around 100 mg per kg and kidney failure from about 175 mg per kg; a 200 mg tablet is enough to hurt a small dog, and cats are harmed at half those doses. Paracetamol is a poison to cats at any dose and is not a pain reliever anyone should give a dog without a vet. Aspirin is toxic to cats and of limited use in dogs.

The veterinary NSAIDs a vet in India reaches for are meloxicam Rx (dogs, cats with great care, cattle, buffalo), carprofen and firocoxib (dogs), tolfenamic acid (dogs, cats, cattle), flunixin (cattle, horses) and phenylbutazone (horses). All are given with food, with the animal drinking normally, and never alongside a steroid or a second NSAID. The warning signs of trouble are vomiting, black or tarry stool, refusing food, and drinking or urinating more than before; any of these means the drug stops and the vet is told the same day.

India has removed four NSAIDs from veterinary use because of what they did to vultures, which died of kidney failure after eating carcasses of treated cattle: diclofenac (withdrawn from veterinary use in 2006, with the formal prohibition under Section 26A of the Drugs and Cosmetics Act gazetted in 2008), ketoprofen and aceclofenac (notification of 31 July 2023), and nimesulide (gazetted in January 2025). Aceclofenac turns into diclofenac inside a cow. Meloxicam is the vulture-safe alternative and is what the Vulture Recovery Plan recommended in place of diclofenac.

2.2Steroids: the most useful and most misused injection in India

Corticosteroids such as prednisolone and dexamethasone Rx suppress inflammation and the immune system. In the right hands they treat allergy, immune-mediated disease, some cancers and shock. In the wrong hands they hide an infection, delay healing and cause a second disease.

Do this
Do thisIf an animal has been on steroids for more than a week or two, never stop suddenly. The vet tapers the dose so the adrenal glands can restart; ask for the taper schedule in writing.

At anti-inflammatory doses (prednisolone around 0.5 to 1 mg per kg a day in dogs) they relieve itch and swelling; at immunosuppressive doses (2 to 4 mg per kg a day) they switch off the immune response, which is intended in immune-mediated anaemia and dangerous in an infection. Predictable effects are thirst, hunger, more urine, panting and a thin coat; longer courses bring muscle loss, a pot belly, skin infections and, in cats, diabetes. Steroids and NSAIDs together ulcerate the gut; this pair is the single most common dangerous combination in animal medicine.

Two Indian specifics. Dexamethasone in the last trimester of a cow or buffalo's pregnancy can bring on calving and abortion. And the "one dexa injection" given to any animal that looks unwell, without a diagnosis, is how a treatable infection is masked until it is untreatable.

Basis
2.3Antiparasitics: dewormers, tick and flea products, and the two that must never touch a cat

Parasites are the commonest treatable disease in Indian animals, and antiparasitic drugs are the class a caregiver is most likely to handle. Most are safe on the label's terms; the deaths come from using a dog product on a cat, or a livestock product on a pet.

WarningDo this
CarefulPermethrin and other concentrated pyrethroids, and amitraz, cause tremors, seizures and death in cats, including a cat that grooms a freshly treated dog. Ivermectin at mange doses can be fatal to Collies, Australian Shepherds, Shelties and their crosses, which carry the MDR1 gene defect; the signs are wobbling, dilated pupils, drooling, blindness and coma. Any dog of unknown breeding treated with ivermectin should be watched, and the drug is a vet's decision.
Do thisChildren who play with a dewormed dog are safer than children who play with one that was not. Roundworm and hookworm pass to people through soil and skin. Pick up faeces and deworm on schedule; this is public health.

Worms. Pyrantel (roundworm, hookworm), praziquantel (tapeworm, including the Echinococcus that causes hydatid disease in people) and fenbendazole (a three-day course; also for Giardia) are the usual dog and cat dewormers OTC in India; combination tablets are dosed by weight band on the label. Puppies are dewormed from two to three weeks of age and every two weeks until weaning, then monthly to six months. Livestock get albendazole, fenbendazole, levamisole, ivermectin, and closantel or triclabendazole for liver fluke in flood-prone districts; the ICAR goat calendar recommends deworming before and after the monsoon and at least fifteen days before vaccinating.

Ticks and fleas. Fipronil spot-ons (dogs and cats), imidacloprid, selamectin, and the newer isoxazoline chews (fluralaner, sarolaner, afoxolaner) Rx, which also treat sarcoptic and demodectic mange in dogs and have made ivermectin injections for mange largely unnecessary. Deltamethrin collars and cattle pour-ons, and amitraz dips, are for dogs and livestock only.

Basis
2.4Antihistamines: what they can and cannot do about an itch

Antihistamines block one messenger of allergy. They help with an acute reaction, a wasp sting, a vaccine lump, mild hives. They do little for the chronic scratching of mange, fleas or food allergy, where the itch has another cause that the drug does not touch.

Do this
Do thisAn animal that scratches for more than a few days needs a cause found, not a stronger antihistamine: look for fleas and ticks, skin scrape for mites, ask about diet. Treating the cause ends the itch; treating the itch alone never does.

The ones on Indian prescriptions are chlorpheniramine, cetirizine and diphenhydramine by mouth, and pheniramine (widely sold as Avil) by injection, which vets use for acute allergic reactions and sometimes to blunt reactions to antivenom. Sedation is the main side effect; in cats chlorpheniramine can cause excitement instead. Human "cold and flu" tablets that combine an antihistamine with a decongestant (pseudoephedrine, phenylephrine) are dangerous to dogs and cats and must not be used.

Basis
2.5Stomach and gut: antiemetics, antacids, and why a vomiting puppy is a same-day case

Vomiting and diarrhoea are symptoms, not diagnoses. The gut drugs on a prescription buy comfort and time while the cause is found and fluids are replaced; they do not cure anything by themselves.

Warning4 facts
CarefulA puppy or an unvaccinated young dog that vomits, refuses food and passes foul or bloody diarrhoea is a parvovirus case until proved otherwise, and parvovirus kills by dehydration within days. That animal needs fluids from a vet today, not an antiemetic tablet and a wait.
AntiemeticsRx: maropitant (an injection or tablet given once a day), ondansetron, metoclopramide. Metoclopramide is not given if a blockage is possible, because it drives the gut against the obstruction.
Acid blockersomeprazole, pantoprazole, famotidine. Useful with NSAID injury, kidney disease and reflux; not a reason to keep an animal on an NSAID that is hurting it.
Sucralfate coats ulcers and must be given two hours away from other drugs, which it also coats.
Antidiarrhoealsloperamide has a narrow place in dogs and none in cats or MDR1 breeds; it is never used when the diarrhoea is infectious or bloody. Kaolin-pectin preparations and probiotics are gentle and of modest effect.

2.6Fluids: reading dehydration, and what may be given by mouth

Dehydration kills more sick animals in India than the diseases that caused it. It is also the one thing a caregiver can measure at the roadside, with two fingers and a watch.

Full explanation

Lift the skin over the shoulder blades and let go. In a hydrated animal it snaps back at once; in a dehydrated one it stands or sinks slowly (the "skin tent"). Press a finger on the gum until it whitens and release: colour should return in under two seconds. Gums that are dry and tacky, eyes that look sunken, and a heart racing faster than the ranges in Lesson 7.2 all point the same way.

What a caregiver may do: offer water, or an oral rehydration solution if a vet has advised it, to an animal that is conscious, can swallow and is not vomiting. Small amounts often, never forced by syringe into a mouth that cannot swallow. What belongs to the vet: fluids under the skin (common for a dehydrated dog or cat that is still drinking a little) and fluids into a vein, where the rate is calculated for the animal's weight and a wrong rate drowns a small animal from the inside.

Basis
2.7Hormones: oxytocin in the dairy, and the contraceptive injection that causes cancer

Two hormone products are misused on Indian animals at scale. One is a crime under the cruelty law; the other is a slow way to give a dog a fatal disease.

Full explanation

Oxytocin Rx is a Schedule H hormone with real uses in labour and in retained placenta, under a vet. Injected into a cow or buffalo to force milk let-down it causes pain, does not make more milk, damages the reproductive tract, and is an offence: Section 12 of the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act 1960 punishes anyone who performs "phooka" or "doom dev" or any operation, including the injection of any substance, to improve lactation in a way injurious to the animal, and the animal is forfeited to the government. Its retail sale has been repeatedly restricted by the Centre for exactly this reason.

Hormonal contraceptive injections for dogs, usually medroxyprogesterone acetate, stop heat cycles for a season. They also cause pyometra (a pus-filled uterus that kills), mammary tumours and diabetes. Sterilisation, which the Animal Birth Control Rules 2023 make the lawful way to manage community dogs, removes the uterus and the risk with it. There is no safe "injection instead of surgery".

2.8The six conditions behind most sick street dogs, and what treating each involves

A colony caregiver will meet the same six diseases over and over. None of them is treated with a single injection, and each has a medicine or a rule that is easy to get wrong.

6 factsCase file
Tick fever.Ehrlichia canis (fever, nosebleeds, bruising, low platelets), Babesia (anaemia, pale gums, dark urine, jaundice) and Anaplasma, all carried by the brown dog tick. Ehrlichiosis is treated with doxycycline Rx for a full 28 days; babesiosis with diminazene or imidocarb injections that only a vet gives, because the margin between the dose and a toxic dose is narrow. Tick control is the prevention.
Parvovirus.Puppies six weeks to six months, sudden vomiting and bloody, foul diarrhoea. No drug kills the virus; survival is fluids, control of vomiting, warmth, and antibiotics because bacteria cross the damaged gut. The virus lives in soil for months; bleach at one part in thirty is what cleans it. The vaccine series prevents it.
Distemper.Cough, discharge, then twitching and seizures weeks later. Supportive care only; a dog that develops fits rarely recovers. The vaccine series prevents it.
Transmissible venereal tumour.A bleeding, cauliflower-like growth on the genitals, spread by mating. It responds well to vincristine Vet only, a cytotoxic drug given into a vein once a week for several weeks; it must not touch skin, and a dose that leaks outside the vein destroys the tissue around it.
Mange.Sarcoptic (intense itch, crusted ear edges and elbows, contagious to people as a short-lived rash) and demodectic (bald patches, little itch, often in the young or the run-down). Isoxazoline chews or ivermectin under a vet, medicated washes, and treating every dog in contact. Neem oil alone does not cure it.
Maggot wounds (myiasis).Fly larvae in any open wound, ear or the vulva of a bitch in heat, worst in the monsoon. Treated by killing and removing every larva, cleaning, dressing, antibiotics and fly control; Lesson 7.4 covers the first aid.
Case file

A 14 kg community dog in October: off food for three days, a nosebleed, small purple spots on the gums. The vet finds a platelet count of 22,000 and starts doxycycline 100 mg, one and a half tablets once a day (about 10 mg per kg) for 28 days, with food, and a tick product.

By day ten the dog is eating and bright. The temptation is to stop. The published course is 28 days because a shorter one lets Ehrlichia persist and relapse, often as the chronic form with bone-marrow failure. The tablet is given with food and a little water because doxycycline burns the gullet if it lodges.

PFA AcademyAntibiotics and resistance

Antibiotics are the medicines most often given to Indian animals without a diagnosis, and the ones whose misuse reaches furthest: into the milk on a doorstep, into the soil of a colony, and into the hospital ward where a resistant bacterium eventually turns up in a person. This module is about using them so that they keep working.

  • State what antibiotics act on and the three common conditions they do not treat.
  • Recognise the antibiotic classes on an Indian veterinary prescription and each one's signature risk.
  • Apply the five rules of a course and explain a withdrawal period to a farmer.
3.1What antibiotics do, and the three things they do not

Antibiotics kill bacteria or stop them multiplying. They have no effect on viruses, on fungi or on parasites, which between them cause most of the fevers, coughs and diarrhoeas a caregiver sees.

Warning
CarefulEvery unnecessary course selects for the bacteria that survive it. The resistant strain does not stay in the dog: it moves through faeces into soil and water and onto hands. Antibiotic stewardship in animals is not a courtesy to farmers; it is human medicine.

Parvovirus, distemper, kennel cough (mostly viral), ringworm (a fungus) and worms are not cured by an antibiotic. Sometimes a vet still prescribes one alongside a viral disease, as in parvovirus, where bacteria cross the damaged gut wall and the antibiotic prevents sepsis. That is a vet's judgement about a specific risk, not a reason to buy amoxicillin for every vomiting puppy.

Bactericidal drugs (penicillins, cephalosporins, fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, metronidazole) kill; bacteriostatic ones (tetracyclines, macrolides, sulfonamides) hold the bacteria still while the immune system finishes them. The distinction matters in an animal whose immune system is not working, and it is one reason the same infection is treated differently in a healthy adult and a starving puppy.

3.3The five rules of a course

Right diagnosis, right drug, right dose, right interval, right duration. Miss one and the course fails or breeds resistance; miss two and the animal is worse off than untreated.

Warning5 steps
CarefulLeftover antibiotics are not a medicine chest. A half-strip saved from one dog's course, given to another for a different infection at a guessed dose, is the textbook way to breed a resistant strain and to miss a diagnosis.
  1. Diagnosis first.An antibiotic is chosen for a bacterium, not for a symptom. "Fever" is not a diagnosis.
  2. Drug fitted to the bacterium and the site.Skin, urine, gut, bone and brain each admit different drugs. The vet's choice of a "weaker" older drug is usually stewardship, not stinginess.
  3. Dose by weight.Under-dosing is the classic resistance-breeder: enough drug to select survivors, not enough to kill them. The tablet made for a 30 kg dog is not "a bit strong" for a 6 kg one; it is five times the dose.
  4. Interval matters.Twice a day means every twelve hours. A drug given at 8 am and 2 pm and then nothing till the next morning spends half its time below the level that works.
  5. Duration as prescribed.The published course length is the shortest one shown to clear the infection. Stopping when the animal looks better leaves the hardiest bacteria alive.

4.1How a vaccine works, and why "one shot" is a myth

A vaccine shows the immune system a harmless version or piece of a germ so that it builds antibodies and memory cells in advance. Protection takes one to two weeks to appear, and for most vaccines it needs more than one dose to become solid and to last.

Full explanation

Live attenuated (modified live) vaccines contain a weakened germ that multiplies a little; they give strong, long immunity from fewer doses but are avoided in pregnant and in very sick animals. Killed (inactivated) vaccines cannot multiply, so they carry an adjuvant to provoke the immune system and need a primary course of two doses and regular boosters. Rabies vaccines for animals in India are killed vaccines.

The reason a puppy needs three or four doses is its mother. Antibodies in the first milk (colostrum) protect a puppy for weeks, and while they last they also neutralise a vaccine before the puppy's own immune system can respond. The level and the fade-out date differ from litter to litter and from puppy to puppy, so a series given every two to four weeks until sixteen weeks of age or later is the only way to be sure one dose lands after the maternal antibodies have gone. A single early dose gives no reliable protection at all.

Core vaccines protect against diseases that every animal of that species should be protected from because they are severe, widespread and often fatal. Non-core vaccines are given on the basis of local risk. In India rabies is core for dogs and cats because the disease is endemic.

4.9Why vaccines fail, and how to tell that one did

A vaccinated animal that gets the disease is almost never proof that the vaccine was bad. It is usually proof of one of six things that happened to the vaccine or the animal.

6 steps
  1. Maternal antibody was still present at the last dose: the series stopped too early, or a puppy was given one shot and called done.
  2. The cold chain broke, somewhere between the factory and the syringe.
  3. The interval was wrongdoses too close together do not add up, and a second dose of a killed vaccine given months late is a first dose again.
  4. The animal could not respondfever, malnutrition, parvovirus, steroids or another immunosuppressive illness at the time.
  5. The vial was expired, counterfeit, or the wrong product for the species.
  6. Exposure came before immunity dida dog bitten the week after its first rabies dose was not yet protected.

For distemper, adenovirus and parvovirus, a serology (titre) test from twenty weeks of age shows whether a puppy responded; WSAVA supports it as an alternative to an extra dose. 5.1The Big Four, and what is in an Indian vial of antivenom

Indian polyvalent anti-snake venom serum is raised in horses against the venoms of the four snakes that cause most deaths in the subcontinent: the spectacled cobra (Naja naja), the common krait (Bungarus caeruleus), Russell's viper (Daboia russelii) and the saw-scaled viper (Echis carinatus).

Full explanation

The standard is written on every vial: one millilitre of reconstituted serum neutralises 0.6 mg of cobra venom, 0.45 mg of krait venom, 0.6 mg of Russell's viper venom and 0.45 mg of saw-scaled viper venom. A vial holds 10 mL. It is made in the public and private sector, among others by the Central Research Institute at Kasauli, Haffkine, Bharat Serums, VINS and Premium Serums, and it is the same product whether the patient is a child or a bullock.

What it does not cover: the king cobra, the hump-nosed pit viper of the Western Ghats and other pit vipers, sea snakes, and other regional species. Venom also varies by region within a species, which is why a Russell's viper bite in one State can respond less well than in another. When the snake is known not to be one of the four, antivenom is a risk without a benefit.

Basis
5.2How antivenom works, and why the dose follows the venom and not the animal

Antivenom is a purified immunoglobulin fragment that binds venom molecules so that they can no longer act. It works only on venom still circulating; damage already done is not undone. So the earlier it is given the more it can do, and the amount needed depends on how much venom the snake injected, not on how large the victim is.

Warning
CarefulAntivenom is given intravenously by a veterinarian. It is never injected at the bite site, never given under the skin or into muscle by a rescuer, and never diluted "to make it go further".

A large viper can inject the same quantity into a 4 kg puppy as into a 60 kg man, and the puppy, with less blood to dilute it, needs at least as much antivenom. The vet judges the starting dose from the species of snake, the speed of the signs and the bedside clotting test, gives it slowly into a vein, and repeats it if bleeding, swelling or weakness keep advancing. There is no correct dose per kilogram of dog, which is why "one vial for a small animal" is not a rule.

Because the serum comes from horses, a minority of patients react to it, from hives to full anaphylaxis, and serum sickness can follow a week later. The vet has adrenaline drawn up before the drip starts; a skin test does not predict who will react. Reaction is a reason for the vet to be ready, not a reason to withhold antivenom from an envenomed animal.

Basis
5.3Recognising envenomation: vipers, cobras and kraits look different

Dogs are bitten on the face and front legs because they investigate; cattle and goats on the muzzle and lower legs because they graze. Not every bite injects venom. What follows in the first hour tells you which snake, and whether to run.

4 factsCase file
Vipers (Russell's, saw-scaled): rapid, painful swelling that spreads up the limb or across the face; oozing from the fang marks; bruising; blood in urine, vomit or from the gums; collapse. Russell's viper attacks the kidneys, and dark urine or none at all in the next day is the sign.
Cobra: pain and local swelling, sometimes a spreading black patch of dead tissue, then drooping eyelids, drooling, weak limbs, difficulty swallowing and slow, shallow breathing.
Krait: almost no local sign. A dog found weak and floppy in the morning, unable to lift its head or breathe deeply, after a night outdoors, is a krait bite until proved otherwise. Kraits bite sleeping animals and people.
Dry bite: fang marks, no progression after an hour or two. Still a vet visit; the decision that no antivenom is needed is the vet's.
Case file

A 10 kg dachshund in Karnataka was brought to a veterinary college hospital with a swollen face, fang marks on the right cheek and blood-tinged fluid oozing from the bite, after a Russell's viper bite. The only abnormal blood value was a low platelet count. It was treated with polyvalent antivenom into a vein, intravenous saline, and supporting drugs for five days, and recovered fully.

The lesson in the report is the order of events: the owner recognised the bite, carried the dog to a hospital that stocked antivenom, and the antivenom went in before the coagulopathy became bleeding.

5.4First aid: what helps in the first ten minutes, and the five measures that harm

The whole of useful snakebite first aid is to keep the animal still, keep it calm, and get it to antivenom. Everything traditionally done at the bite site delays that and most of it makes the injury worse.

Do and never

Do

  • Move the animal away from the snake without chasing or handling the snake; note its colour and pattern, or photograph it from a distance if that costs no time. Snakes are protected under the Wild Life (Protection) Act 1972; do not kill it.
  • Keep the animal as still as possible and carry it. A dog that walks pumps venom through its lymphatics faster.
  • Take off the collar; a face or neck bite swells within minutes.
  • Note the time of the bite and go straight to the nearest vet or veterinary hospital that stocks antivenom. Telephone ahead so the vial is out of the fridge.
  • If the animal stops breathing and you know how, support its breathing on the way. Krait and cobra kill by paralysing the breathing muscles, and antivenom reverses it.

Don't

  • Tie a tourniquet or a tight cord above the bite. It causes gangrene and does not stop viper venom.
  • Cut the bite or try to suck venom out. Suction removes almost none and the cut bleeds in an animal that may soon be unable to clot.
  • Apply ice, cold water, hot packs or electric shock. Ice worsens tissue death.
  • Apply herbs, "snake stones", lime, kerosene or any paste, or give alcohol or a home remedy by mouth.
  • Wait to see whether symptoms develop. By the time they are obvious, the antivenom has more to do and less time.

PFA AcademyImmunoglobulins: borrowed immunity

An immunoglobulin is an antibody given ready-made. It works within hours and fades within weeks, which makes it the right tool for the day of a rabies bite, a snakebite, a deep wound in a horse, or a newborn's first meal, and the wrong tool for anything long-term. 6.1Passive and active immunity: hours versus years

A vaccine teaches the body to make its own antibodies: slow to start, long to last. An immunoglobulin lends antibodies made elsewhere: immediate, and gone in weeks. Rabies prophylaxis uses both at once, for exactly that reason.

Full explanation

After a serious bite, rabies immunoglobulin infiltrated into the wound neutralises virus on day zero, while the vaccine given the same day takes about a fortnight to raise the person's own antibodies to the protective level of 0.5 IU per mL. The immunoglobulin covers the gap. Given too late, or in too large a dose, it can blunt the vaccine's response, which is why the national guidelines fix its window and its maximum.

Sources of borrowed antibody: horses (equine rabies immunoglobulin, antivenom, tetanus antitoxin), pooled human plasma (human rabies immunoglobulin), laboratory-made monoclonal antibodies, and the mother, through colostrum.

6.2Rabies post-exposure prophylaxis for the person who was bitten: the whole protocol

In India every bite by a warm-blooded animal is treated as a possible rabies exposure, and post-exposure prophylaxis is a medical emergency, not something to arrange next week. Rabies is fatal once symptoms begin and entirely preventable before they do.

Do this
Do thisIf you feed or handle community animals, take pre-exposure prophylaxis now: one dose intramuscularly or intradermally on days 0, 7 and 21 or 28. It turns any later bite into a two-dose visit with no immunoglobulin, and it buys time when a bite happens far from a clinic.

Step one, wound washing. Soap or detergent and running water for ten to fifteen minutes, then an antiseptic such as povidone-iodine or alcohol. This alone removes much of the virus. Do not touch the wound with bare hands, do not apply soil, chillies, oil, lime, herbs, chalk or betel leaves (the guidelines' own list), nor turmeric or salt, and do not get it stitched if that can be avoided; if it must be sutured, the immunoglobulin goes in first and the stitches wait a few hours.

Step two, the category. Category I (touching, licks on intact skin) needs no prophylaxis. Category II (nibbling of bare skin, minor scratches without bleeding) needs the vaccine. Category III (any bite that breaks the skin, scratches that bleed, licks on broken skin or on the eyes, nose or mouth, and any bite from a wild animal) needs the vaccine and rabies immunoglobulin.

Step three, the immunoglobulin (Category III, once, on day 0, no later than day 7 of the vaccine course). Equine RIG at 40 IU per kg (Indian ERIG is 300 IU per mL, maximum 3000 IU), or human RIG at 20 IU per kg, or a rabies monoclonal antibody, Rabishield (approved 2016, 3.33 IU per kg) or TwinRab (approved 2019, 40 IU per kg). As much as possible is infiltrated into and around every wound; the rest goes deep into muscle away from the vaccine site. Never in the same syringe or the same limb as the vaccine.

Step four, the vaccine. Either the intramuscular Essen schedule, one dose in the deltoid (thigh in small children, never the buttock) on days 0, 3, 7, 14 and 28, or the intradermal schedule, 0.1 mL at two sites on days 0, 3, 7 and 28, at an anti-rabies clinic that uses it. Someone previously fully vaccinated needs only two doses, on days 0 and 3, and no immunoglobulin. Tetanus toxoid and, if the wound warrants it, an antibiotic are added.

The dog. A dog or cat that can be observed is watched for ten days. If it stays healthy the course may be shortened by the doctor; if it sickens or dies, the course is completed and the animal's head is sent for testing through the veterinary authority. Prophylaxis is never delayed while waiting to see.

6.4Tetanus antitoxin and toxoid: the horse, the wound and the fortnight

Tetanus antitoxin is equine immunoglobulin against the tetanus toxin. Given to a wounded animal that has no vaccine history, it protects for about two weeks, which is long enough for a wound to close and for the toxoid to begin working.

Full explanation

Horses and donkeys are by far the most susceptible domestic animals; cattle, sheep and goats less so; dogs and cats are relatively resistant and rarely need it. The classic cases are a nail in a hoof, a castration or docking done with a dirty blade, a foaling or a deep puncture. The vet gives antitoxin and the first dose of toxoid at the same visit in different sites, then the toxoid course continues (Lesson 4.7). The signs of tetanus itself, stiffness, a locked jaw, a raised tail and the third eyelid flicking across the eye at a noise, appear days to weeks after the wound, and by then treatment is long and often fails.

6.5Colostrum: the immunoglobulin every newborn must drink in its first hours

Calves, kids, lambs and foals are born with almost no antibodies of their own. Everything that protects them for their first months arrives in the first milk, and the gut can absorb it whole only in the first day of life.

Full explanation

The rule of thumb for a calf is colostrum within the first two hours, and about ten per cent of its body weight over the first day: roughly three to four litres for a 35 kg crossbred calf, in several feeds. By twenty-four hours the gut has closed and antibodies drunk after that are digested as protein, not absorbed. A calf that missed its colostrum is the calf that dies of scours or pneumonia at three weeks. Kids and lambs are the same in miniature; the ICAR goat calendar credits colostrum with protecting kids for their first three months.

Puppies and kittens absorb antibody for their first two to three days. Orphans who missed it are the ones a vet may put on plasma or a hyperimmune serum, and who need their vaccine series started on time. Cow's milk is not a substitute for any of them; a species-appropriate replacer or, for a puppy, a puppy milk replacer, is.

6.6Hyperimmune sera for parvo and distemper: what the evidence supports

Indian clinics can buy hyperimmune serum or immunoglobulin products raised against canine parvovirus, distemper and adenovirus, sold as an adjunct in the first days of infection. The evidence that they change survival is thin, and they are never a substitute for fluids.

Full explanation

The theory is sound, borrowed antibody against a virus in a puppy that has none, and the products are used by many vets in the first 48 hours of parvovirus. Controlled evidence of benefit is limited, and a puppy that gets serum and no drip still dies of dehydration. The honest position is that they may help early, alongside intensive supportive care, and that the money is better spent on the vaccine series that would have prevented the illness.

6.7Maternal antibody, again: the reason for every vaccine series

The same borrowed antibody that saves a puppy in its first weeks is what neutralises its early vaccines. Understanding that one fact removes most of the confusion about puppy and kitten schedules.

Full explanation

Maternal antibody falls by half roughly every week to ten days, but the starting level depends on the mother's own immunity and how much colostrum the animal drank. So in one litter a puppy may be open to vaccination at eight weeks and its sibling not until fourteen. The series every two to four weeks until sixteen weeks or older, and the extra dose at twenty-six weeks, exist to catch every puppy on the far side of its own fade-out. It is not over-vaccination; it is the only honest answer to an unknown date.

7.6Heatstroke: cool first, transport second

Heatstroke kills by cooking organs, and every minute above 41 °C counts. The current veterinary emergency guidance reverses the old advice: cool the animal aggressively where it is, then transport. Wet towels laid over the body and "tepid, not cold, water" are outdated and slow.

Warning5 steps
CarefulNo wet towels over the body (they trap heat once they warm), no ice packs to the skin, no ice baths for old or sick animals. For cattle and buffalo, shade, water sprinkled over the body and fans; a buffalo denied a wallow in May is a heatstroke waiting to happen.
  1. Into shade. Water over the whole body at once: any water cooler than the dog, and for a young, healthy dog, immersion in cold water is the fastest method.
  2. Air movement: a fan, an air-conditioned car, a breeze. Evaporation does the cooling.
  3. Offer water to drink if the animal is conscious; do not force it.
  4. Keep cooling on the way to the vet and stop active cooling when the rectal temperature reaches about 39.4 °C, to avoid overshooting into hypothermia.
  5. Vet regardless of recovery: kidney and gut damage and clotting failure appear over the next day.

A dog collapses on a June afternoon, panting, gums brick red, rectal temperature 41.5 °C. The vet is thirty minutes away. What do you do first?

"Cool first, transport second" is the current emergency guidance. Wet towels over the body trap heat once they warm and are far less effective than water and airflow or immersion.

A cow has eaten a packet of rat-poison cakes (bromadiolone). She looks completely normal two hours later. What is the correct conclusion?

Anticoagulant rodenticides act on clotting factors and the signs appear two to five days later. Early vitamin K1 treatment, guided by the exact product, prevents the bleeding. Salt is never used to induce vomiting.

You find a cold, limp newborn puppy on a winter morning. In what order do you act?

A chilled newborn cannot digest; feeding it first causes bloat and death. Warm slowly, then feed the right milk. Plain cow's milk causes diarrhoea in puppies and kittens.

PFA AcademyDosages: the arithmetic

A dose is a weight of drug for a weight of animal, converted into a number of tablets or millilitres. Every step in that chain can be checked with a calculator, and every step is where overdoses come from: a guessed body weight, a decimal point, a tablet of the wrong strength, "twice a day" given four hours apart. This module is the arithmetic, so that a caregiver can follow a prescription exactly and catch a mistake before the animal does.

  • Convert a mg/kg dose into tablets or millilitres for a given animal and strength, and check someone else's conversion.
  • Weigh or estimate the weight of a dog, a calf and a cow well enough to dose them.
  • Read the label ranges of the common medicines and recognise a prescription that sits outside them.
8.1Milligrams per kilogram, and the body weight everything hangs on

Almost every animal dose is written per kilogram of body weight, because a drug spreads through the animal's blood and tissue and a bigger animal has more of both. So the first number in any dose is not on the prescription at all: it is the weight of the animal, and a guessed weight makes a guessed dose.

Warning3 facts
CarefulFat and fluid do not take drugs the way muscle does. A vet doses some medicines to the animal's lean weight, and a starving dog with a swollen belly of fluid is lighter, for dosing, than the scale says. When in doubt the vet decides which weight to use.
Dogs and cats: stand on a bathroom scale holding the animal, then without it, and subtract. For a puppy or kitten, a kitchen scale in a bowl; for a large dog, a luggage scale and a sling. A colony caregiver who weighs each dog once a season has a number that a vet can use on the telephone.
Calves, goats: the same luggage-scale sling, or a weighbridge at the cooperative.
Cattle and buffalo without a scale: a tape. Measure the heart girth in centimetres, just behind the front legs, and the body length from the point of the shoulder to the pin bone. Weight in kilograms is close to girth squared, times length, divided by 10,840 (Schaeffer's formula in metric units). A cow with a 170 cm girth and a 150 cm length weighs about 170 × 170 × 150 / 10,840, which is about 400 kg.
Basis
8.2From mg/kg to tablets and millilitres: three worked examples and the decimal point

Three steps, always the same: multiply the weight by the dose per kilogram to get the milligrams; divide the milligrams by the strength to get the tablets or the millilitres; check that the answer is something you can actually measure.

3 worked examples
Example one: a tablet

A 12 kg dog, doxycycline 5 mg/kg twice a day, 100 mg tablets. 12 × 5 = 60 mg per dose. 60 ÷ 100 = 0.6 of a tablet. Half a tablet is 50 mg (4.2 mg/kg); the vet may round to half, or choose 10 mg/kg once a day, which is 120 mg, near enough one tablet. Either way the rounding is the vet's, and it stays inside the published range.

Example two: a liquid

A 20 kg dog, meloxicam 0.1 mg/kg once a day, oral suspension 1.5 mg/mL. 20 × 0.1 = 2 mg. 2 ÷ 1.5 = 1.33 mL, drawn up in a syringe. Misread the dose as 1 mg/kg and the answer becomes 13.3 mL, ten times the dose, and a dog with a bleeding stomach and failing kidneys. The decimal point is the most dangerous character on a prescription.

Example three: a livestock injection

A 400 kg cow, ivermectin 0.2 mg/kg under the skin, 1 per cent injection (10 mg/mL). 400 × 0.2 = 80 mg. 80 ÷ 10 = 8 mL. The label says the same thing as "1 mL per 50 kg". If the cow was guessed at 300 kg, she gets 6 mL and the worms survive; guessed at 500 kg, she gets 10 mL, which for this drug is tolerated, but the same guess with a narrow-margin drug is not.

Two things that can never be measured: less than a quarter of a tablet, and less than a tenth of a millilitre in an ordinary syringe. If the arithmetic produces one of these, the strength is wrong for the animal, and the answer is a lower-strength tablet or a liquid, not a knife and a guess.

8.3Frequency and duration: what "twice a day" means, and the missed-dose rule

A dose is designed to keep the drug above a working level until the next one. "Twice a day" means every twelve hours, not "morning and evening whenever". Doses bunched together overdose; doses spread out under-treat.

4 facts
Fix the clock times at the start of a course and keep them: 8 and 8, or 7 and 7. Set alarms. A colony caregiver with several dogs on courses needs a written chart.
Missed dose: give it as soon as you remember, unless the next dose is due within a few hours, in which case skip it and carry on. Never double a dose to catch up.
Duration: the number of days on the prescription is the course. Doxycycline for tick fever is 28 days because shorter courses relapse; a five-day antibiotic for a skin infection is five days because that is what was tested. Stopping early because the animal looks better is the commonest reason an infection returns worse.
Long courses of steroids, thyroid or heart medicines are not stopped when the tablets run out; they are refilled, or tapered on the vet's instruction.

8.6Livestock: pour-ons, drenches, injections and "one bottle per cow"

Farm medicines are dosed per kilogram like any other, but they are bought in bottles sized for a herd, which is how a habit of "one bottle for a big cow, half for a small one" grows. The label converts the dose into millilitres per 50 kg or per 10 kg; the tape and the calculator do the rest.

5 facts
Drenches (oral dewormers) are given with a drenching gun or a bottle over the tongue, slowly, head level, to an animal that is standing. Drench forced into a struggling animal goes into the lungs.
Pour-ons are measured for the weight and poured along the backline; they are absorbed through the skin and the dose is not "until it looks wet".
Injections go where the label says: under the skin of the neck, or into the neck muscle; large volumes are split between sites. The rump is avoided in meat animals.
Withdrawal is written down for milk and for meat, animal by animal (Lesson 3.6).
Deworming schedules follow the season: the ICAR goat calendar deworms before and after the monsoon, and treats for liver fluke in flood-prone districts.

8.7Why the same drug has a different dose in a different species

Dose per kilogram is not a property of the drug alone. It is the drug and the animal together, and the same molecule can need ten times more in one species than in another, or be forbidden altogether.

5 facts
Enrofloxacin: dogs up to 20 mg/kg a day; cats 5 mg/kg, because of their retina.
Pyrantel: dogs 5 mg/kg; cats 20 mg/kg, because cats absorb and clear it differently.
Xylazine, the sedative used in large-animal practice: cattle need roughly a tenth of the horse's dose per kilogram; a horse dose sedates a cow into respiratory arrest.
Ivermectin: routine in cattle at 0.2 mg/kg; the same dose in a Collie can be fatal.
Paracetamol: a human standby, a narrow-margin drug in dogs, a poison in cats.

The rule that follows: a dose you know for one species is not a dose you know for another, and a prescription written for one animal is not a prescription for its neighbour of a different species and size.

PFA AcademyIndications, contraindications and interactions

An indication is what a medicine is for. A contraindication is the animal, or the condition, in which it must not be given. An interaction is what two medicines do to each other inside the same animal. A vet holds all three in mind before writing a line; a caregiver who understands them can supply the facts the vet needs and question a combination that looks wrong.

  • Use the words indication, off-label, contraindication and interaction correctly.
  • Check a prescription against the animal's pregnancy, age, breed, species, kidneys and other medicines.
  • Give the food and timing rules that change whether a dose works.
9.1Indication, off-label, contraindication, side effect: the four words

Four words carry most of the safety of medicine, and they are used loosely in conversation. Here they are used exactly.

4 facts
Indication: the disease or condition a drug is licensed and shown to treat. Doxycycline is indicated for ehrlichiosis; it is not indicated for a viral cough.
Off-label (extra-label): use of a licensed medicine for a species, condition, dose or route not on its label, on a vet's professional judgement. It is common, lawful and often the only option, because few drugs are licensed for every species in India; ivermectin for canine mange and most cat medicines are off-label. It is a vet's decision because it rests on evidence the label does not carry.
Contraindication: a reason the drug must not be given. Absolute (permethrin in cats, live vaccine in a sick animal) or relative (an NSAID in an old dog whose kidneys have not been checked), where the vet weighs the risk against the need.
Side effect, toxicity, allergy: three different things. A side effect is an expected, dose-related nuisance (thirst on steroids). Toxicity is harm from too much (paracetamol in a cat). An allergy is the immune system reacting to any dose (hives after a vaccine). The response to each is different, which is why a vet asks exactly what happened.

An animal that reacts badly to a medicine needs two things: treatment for the reaction, and a record that lets the next animal avoid it.

Thirty sentences. The Do's are about weight, records, timing and the vet. The Don'ts are about the kitchen, the cupboard and the neighbour.

Do and never

Do

  • Weigh the animal. Every dose starts there.
  • Read the molecule, the strength and the weight aloud before every first dose.
  • Give a prescribed course exactly: dose, route, clock time, number of days.

  • Vaccinate in series, on schedule, from a cold vial, into a healthy animal; write down the batch.

Don't

  • Give a human painkiller to a dog or cat. Paracetamol kills cats; ibuprofen and diclofenac kill dogs.

10.3Ten sentences that get a vet to help you fast

10 steps

  2. "What happened: hit by a two-wheeler at about 9 pm" (or: found weak this morning; bitten by a snake; ate rat poison).
  3. "Now it is: conscious but not standing, breathing fast, about 60 a minute."
  4. "Gums are pale pink and slow to refill" (or white, or blue, or red).
  5. "Temperature is 40.3" (or: I could not take it).
  6. "Bleeding: from the left hind leg, controlled with pressure" (or none seen).
  7. "Vomiting or diarrhoea: vomited twice, no blood."
  8. "Given so far: nothing" (or exactly what, how much, and when).
  9. "History: vaccinated for rabies in March, on doxycycline since last week, no known illness."
  10. "I can be at the clinic in twenty minutes; what should I do on the way?"

Then listen, and do what the vet says on the way, including the things that feel like doing nothing: keeping the animal flat, warm, quiet and unfed.

10.4Where this Academy stops, and the law starts

This page teaches how to read and follow medicine, not how to practise it. Three laws draw the line, and knowing them protects the caregiver as much as the animal.

3 facts
The Indian Veterinary Council Act 1984 reserves veterinary practice, including choosing drugs and doses, to registered veterinary practitioners. Following a prescription and giving first aid are not practice.
The Animal Birth Control Rules 2023 make sterilisation and vaccination under a veterinarian the lawful way to manage community dogs, and give caregivers a recognised role in presenting dogs and keeping records.
The Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act 1960 makes cruelty, including injurious injections to force lactation, an offence; a person who sees it can report it through the Laws page on this site.

A vet who is asked good questions gives better care. That is the whole purpose of the ten modules above.

What changed, 2019 to 2026

The rules moved. The habits have not caught up.

Six changes that a caregiver, a farmer or a village vet may not have heard of, each of which alters what is safe or lawful to do today.

Nimesulide banned for animals, 2025

The fourth NSAID removed from veterinary use for the sake of vultures, after diclofenac (2006), ketoprofen and aceclofenac (2023). Meloxicam is the lawful alternative in cattle.

Colistin out of the food chain, 2019

Prohibited for food-producing animals, poultry, aquaculture and feed. A last-resort human antibiotic that Indian poultry had been eating as a growth promoter.

Animal Birth Control Rules, 2023

Sterilisation plus anti-rabies vaccination, ear-notching and return became the lawful management of community dogs, with a recognised role for caregivers.

WSAVA vaccination guidelines, 2024

The puppy and kitten series now ends at 16 weeks or later with an extra dose at 26 weeks; core boosters no more than every three years; leukaemia vaccine recommended for kittens and outdoor cats.

Rabies monoclonal antibodies, 2016 and 2019

Rabishield and TwinRab give India two lab-made alternatives to horse and human rabies immunoglobulin, with fixed doses per kilogram and no serum reactions.

Heatstroke: cool first, transport second

Emergency guidance reversed the old advice. Wet towels and "tepid water" are out; water over the whole body, airflow, and immersion for a young healthy dog are in.
